The Inequality of Recession

Modifying unemployment-based recession indicators using disaggregated metrics, such as for labor utilization measures, education, race, and gender, creates more responsive indicators that can also be used to reveal labor market disparities. Adjusting the Sahm Rule can also highlight how vulnerable groups experience downturns earlier and more intensely, providing equitable insights for recession detection and policy design.

The evidence

From labor-market disparities to a more holistic understanding of recessions

Can we better indicate recessions? Do recessions hit all people, all the same, all of the time? Or do some people get hit first, are those recessions more harsh, are they longer? And can we bend the tools we use to call recessions, not just to be more rigorous but to speak more meaningfully to underlying economic well-being?

The paper takes a tool macroeconomists already rely on, turning-point detection for calling recessions, and applies it to a broader definition of labor-market data. Differences across groups have usually been described as labor-market disparities: one group’s unemployment is higher, or rises faster. Turning points make the claim more specific. By the same standard used to date the national cycle, particular groups of workers and particular parts of the economy can be in recession while the aggregate is not.

Why it matters

Recessions are understood as uniform, binary events. They are dynamic.

The business-cycle chronology marks one peak and one trough for the whole country, and a recession is either on or off. Turning-point indicators built from disaggregated data show something more dynamic: by the same real-time standard, the recession begins months earlier for some workers and places, cuts deeper, spreads unevenly, and lingers past the official end. Aggregate-triggered measures, from extended unemployment benefits to policy-rate responses, are calibrated to the national date, so they tend to arrive after the groups most exposed have already been living in a downturn.

The indicators on this site update with each release: BLS series for the national and group rates, county unemployment from the Local Area Unemployment Statistics, and CPS microdata through IPUMS for the income and composition analyses. The interactive tool lets readers rebuild any of these signals with their own smoothing, window and threshold choices, and the code for the tool and the map is on GitHub.

The series

Multiple papers, one question: who does a recession happen to, and when?

Each paper takes one dimension of inequality and asks the same three questions: when does the recession begin for this group, how deep does it go, and how long does it last relative to the official dates? The racial paper asks how much of Black workers’ earlier and longer recession remains once income, education, age, sex and region are accounted for. The income paper follows family-income deciles, the educational paper compares credentials, and the spatial paper moves from groups of people to places, following county unemployment.

Read together, the papers are less about any single gap than about timing. A downturn that the national chronology dates to one month is, for many workers and communities, already under way before it, and often still under way after the official trough.

Contributions

What the paper adds

A generalized framework

The Modified Sahm Rule does more than treat the Sahm Rule’s conventions (smoothing, window, threshold, series) as parameters. It places them in a turning-point framework, open to any labor-market input, consistent with how the NBER’s Business Cycle Dating Committee already thinks about recessions: peaks and troughs judged by depth, diffusion and duration.

More comprehensive signals

Running one rule across many measures gives more than an earlier or broader alarm. Broad measures of labor utilization such as U-6 move before headline unemployment, consistent with hours being cut before workers are laid off. Read together, the indicators trace the contours of the labor market as a downturn arrives, deepens and then recedes.

How recessions evolve

Recessions appear not as binary, on-or-off events but as dynamic, compositional ones. By race, education, income and place, the same rule shows them reaching vulnerable groups first, hitting them harder and leaving them last: disparate impacts with different causes, different effects and different solutions. Noisier group signals are read alongside one another.

Why timing

Last hired, first fired, and first to know

Black unemployment has run at roughly twice the White rate for as long as the series has existed. That gap in levels is well documented. This paper asks a different question: whether the timing of a recession differs, so that Black workers’ labor market turns down before the aggregate does and recovers after it.

If it does, an indicator built on the headline rate is, in effect, waiting for a recession to reach White workers before it calls one. The paper traces that lag across every recession since 1982 and asks how much of it is about race itself.

Reading the split

Composition is what a group’s mix explains; the residual is what it does not

The composition piece prices Black workers’ own mix of income, education, sex, age and region at White workers’ unemployment rates within each of those cells. It answers: if Black workers faced White workers’ labor market, cell by cell, how much earlier would their recession still start?

The residual is what remains. It is not a direct measure of discrimination, since it also absorbs anything the cells leave out, but it is an upper bound on how much of the timing gap the observed characteristics can explain away.

Who sees it first

The bottom of the distribution is the leading edge

Lower-income workers are concentrated in cyclically sensitive jobs and absorb hours cuts and early separations first, so their unemployment departs from its trough before the aggregate does.

Read from the bottom up, the income distribution is an early-warning system. By the time the headline rate has moved enough to trigger a conventional indicator, the poorest tenth of families has often been in a recession for months.

What a credential buys

Time, not only safety

A degree lowers the chance of losing a job in a downturn. It also changes when the downturn arrives. Workers without a high school diploma tend to see their indicator turn first, while those with a bachelor’s degree are often the last to register a recession and the first to leave it.

That ordering matters for policy timing: support keyed to the headline rate reaches the least-educated workers after their recession is already under way.

Inequality of Recession

Recessions also arrive on different dates in different places. Running the rule on county unemployment shows where the downturn began first, and where it lingered.

Why place

A recession spreads across the map

The other papers in this series sort people: by income, by race, by education. This one sorts places. The Bureau of Labor Statistics estimates an unemployment rate every month for each of the country’s 3,136 counties, and every one of those series can be run through the same turning-point rule. Watched this way, a recession spreads across the map rather than switching on everywhere at once. Some counties turn early, some late, and some register downturns that never add up to a national recession.

Three patterns stand out. A handful of counties call recessions cleanly and almost never cry wolf. Counties that turn early tend to raise more false alarms, the same trade-off between speed and accuracy that governs national recession indicators. And places inherit the seasons of the industries they host, strongly enough to fake a recession even after standard seasonal adjustment.

Bellwether counties

A few places call recessions cleanly

Since 1993, 22 counties have crossed the recession threshold in each of the last three national recessions, 2001, 2007–09 and 2020, and at no other time. Many sit in North Carolina’s Piedmont, around Charlotte, Greensboro and Raleigh–Durham; others are suburbs of New York, Philadelphia and Omaha. Their mix of industries is close to the nation’s, so their labor markets move with the national cycle rather than with one plant, crop or tourist season.

Bellwethers are accurate but not early. On average they cross the threshold 2.5 months after the official peak. That is still well before the NBER announces a peak, which took four to twelve months after each of the last three recessions.

Speed against accuracy

Early warning comes with false alarms

Some counties turn many months before the national peak. The cost is false alarms. A county whose labor market rides on a single plant, a commodity price or a tourist season will often cross the threshold early in a real recession, but it will also cross it when nothing national is happening. Pascal Michaillat calls the best attainable combinations of the two the anticipation–precision frontier: for any given lead, the most accurate a signal can be, and for any given accuracy, the earliest warning it can give.

For the typical county the penalty for speed is modest. Among counties that turned six months or more before the peak, 39 percent of signals were real recessions, against 44 percent for counties that turned close to it. At the frontier the trade-off is steep: every extra month of warning costs accuracy, and no county is both very early and always right.

Greater Washington

Slow, but sure

The suburbs of Washington, D.C., show the trade-off in its purest form. Fairfax, Loudoun and Prince William counties in Virginia crossed the threshold in each of the last three recessions, but about 4 months after the official peak, later than most of the country. When they did cross, it was almost always real: 75 percent of their signals came near a national recession, against about 40 percent for the typical county. Across the whole metropolitan area, signals arrive later than in 74 percent of counties and are more accurate than in 91 percent.

The reason is likely the same on both sides of the trade-off. Many residents of Northern Virginia and suburban Maryland work for the federal government or for the contractors, consultants and services that depend on it. That work is insulated from the private-sector shocks that start most recessions, so a downturn reaches these counties late. And because only a broad, national downturn gets through that insulation, a signal from these counties is rarely a false alarm. The exception proves the rule: the only false alarms these three counties have sounded since 1993 came in late 2025, when the shock came from the federal government itself.

The District of Columbia proper behaves differently. Its unemployment rate is higher and more volatile, and only 33 percent of its signals were real. The accuracy belongs to the suburbs. At the opposite corner sits Elkhart County, Indiana, where most of the country’s recreational vehicles are built. RV sales are among the first things households cut when times turn, so Elkhart’s indicator crossed the threshold 11.7 months before the national peak on average. But only 55 percent of its signals were real: the same exposure to the private-sector cycle that makes it fast makes it jumpy.

Recessions the aggregate misses

A region can be in recession while the nation is not

Not every false alarm is noise. Some are real local recessions that never add up to a national one. Every Louisiana parish in the data crossed the threshold in 2014–15 after oil prices collapsed, and most of Wyoming’s counties in 2016, while the national unemployment rate kept falling. In November 2024, 55 percent of all counties were above the threshold, the highest share at any time since 1993 that was not during or just after a national recession, yet no national recession followed. Maryland’s counties were above it from early 2024 into 2026, and the District from early 2025 to early 2026.

Because these episodes are tied to places, policy can cause them and can also cure them. A state budget cut, a regulatory change or a federal decision about where jobs sit can start a local recession; state unemployment insurance, state spending and economic development can cushion one. A national indicator averages all of this away. A county view shows where a downturn is concentrated, which is the information a governor or a state legislature needs.

Seasonality

Places carry the seasons of the industries they host

A county’s labor market reflects the people who live there and, through them, the industries that employ them. Resort, farming, fishing and college towns have seasons that national data average out. BLS does not seasonally adjust county unemployment, and on the unadjusted series the seasons look like recessions: 39 percent of false alarms begin in January or February, after holiday hiring ends and outdoor work stops for the winter.

Standard seasonal adjustment helps but does not finish the job. After the same adjustment the site applies to every series, false alarms still cluster in the first months of the year (22 percent in January and February, against 17 percent if they were spread evenly), and the most seasonal fifth of counties, such as Skagway, Alaska and Mackinac, Michigan, still raise 2.3 times as many false alarms as the least seasonal fifth. Seasonal patterns that shift over time, as industries grow and shrink, escape a fixed seasonal adjustment. County data, and the sector data behind them, need seasonal adjustment designed for them rather than borrowed from national patterns.

Six ways to count slack

What each measure adds

Each U-measure widens the one before it. U-1 and U-2 are narrower than the official rate; U-4 through U-6 add people the official rate leaves out: workers too discouraged to search, people who want a job but have not looked recently, and workers who are employed but working fewer hours than they want. U-4, U-5 and U-6 begin in 1994, when the redesigned Current Population Survey made them possible, so they cover the 2001, 2007–09 and 2020 recessions.

Hours before jobs

The intensive margin moves first

When demand weakens, firms usually cut hours before they cut jobs: shifts shrink, overtime disappears and full-time workers are moved to part time. Those workers are still employed, so the official rate does not count them, but U-6 does. Layoffs follow later, and the long-term unemployment captured by U-1 builds last.

The workers whose hours are cut first are disproportionately the same workers the rest of this site shows entering recession first: lower-income, less-educated, younger, Black and Hispanic workers. U-6 is, in effect, a composite of the leading edge of the labor market.

What U-6 adds to U-3

Discouraged workers, or shorter hours?

U-6 differs from the official rate in two steps: U-5 adds discouraged and other marginally attached workers, and U-6 then adds people working part time for economic reasons. The split below asks how much of U-6’s head start over U-3 comes from each step.

The answer is clear. In every recession since 1994, U-5 crossed the threshold in the same month as U-3: counting discouraged workers adds no early warning. All of U-6’s head start over U-3 comes from adding workers whose hours were cut. As the decomposition below shows, though, much of that is because adding them makes the series larger.

Decomposition

Scale, composition, or the intensive margin?

U-6 is about one and a half to two times the size of U-3, so the same 0.5-point rise is easier for it to reach. It also gives more weight to the groups that are most often underemployed. To separate these from genuinely earlier movement, the split below rebuilds U-3 and a U-6-style rate from 32 race, sex and education cells of the CPS microdata and asks three questions. How much earlier would U-3 fire if it were simply as large as U-6 (scale)? How much earlier if each group’s U-3 were scaled by that group’s own underemployment (composition)? And how much earlier does the actual U-6-style rate fire than that (within groups, the intensive margin)?

Most of U-6’s head start at a fixed threshold is scale. Composition adds little, and within groups part-time work does not consistently move before unemployment. When the threshold is scaled to each measure’s level (the Threshold scaling control in the full analysis below), U-6 and U-3 fire within a month of each other. The case for U-6 is less that it is faster than that it is larger: it shows how much more slack a recession creates, and for whom.

The argument

Broad measures pool the margins

Recessions reach some workers first. On this site’s other pages, unemployment among Black workers, young workers, workers without a diploma and lower-income households turns up before the national rate. A rule built on any one of those series also sounds more false alarms, because the samples are small, the series are noisy, and some shocks belong to one group or one industry rather than to the economy. A broad measure such as U-6 pools those margins without inheriting all of that noise. It reaches the workers who feel a downturn first because it counts more kinds of underutilization, not because it singles out any one group.

If breadth is the virtue, how far can it go? The ladder below adds four rungs to the official six, all built from the public CPS microdata and ordered from the narrowest to the broadest, jobless people first: everyone else outside the labor force who says they want a job (U-7); the own-account and the incorporated self-employed, two margins of independent and gig work (U-8 and U-9); and, at the top, workers whose degree is above what their occupation typically requires (U-10), the skills-mismatch measure in the research proposal. From U-3 up, each rung counts everyone the rung below it counts, and no one twice. The two narrowest official measures, U-1 and U-2, are slices of U-3, the long-term unemployed and job losers, so they can cross each other.

Breadth or narrowness

Earlier warnings, more false alarms

The chart places every measure on the speed–accuracy frontier from the spatial page: how early its signal comes, on average, against the share of its signals that were real recessions. The narrow series sit low and to the right: unemployment among Black workers, young workers, workers without a diploma and immigrants turns up early but rings falsely more often. The official rate sits high and to the left, late but right every time since 1994, and so do U-1 and U-2, the narrowest official measures. With three recessions and a handful of signals per series, one false alarm moves a measure by 15 to 25 points, so the pattern, not the ranking, is the finding. Several of the false alarms are recent: the broad measures crossed the threshold in 2024 and 2025, and, as of these data, the NBER has not dated a recession since 2020.

Next

What the restricted data would add

The public CPS can build the mismatch rung, but only coarsely. Occupations come in … harmonized categories, education stops at the highest degree, there is no field of study, and subgroup samples are too small for monthly mismatch series by race or by place. The restricted American Community Survey and LEHD files in the proposal would sharpen the level and the distribution of mismatch: detailed occupations, field of degree, local labor markets, and earnings records to test whether mismatched workers are in fact paid less, and move on when they can.

They will not sharpen the timing. The ACS is annual and LEHD quarterly, so monthly turning points still have to come from the CPS. The public-data ladder on this page is the monthly companion to the restricted-data measure.

U-1 to U-6 are the official seasonally adjusted series. U-1 and U-2 are slices of U-3, the long-term unemployed and job losers; they overlap and can cross each other. From U-3 up, each rung adds one more group to the numerator and, when the group is outside the labor force, to the denominator too, jobless groups first. A worker who fits two groups is counted once, in the lower rung: a self-employed worker who is part time for economic reasons is already in U-6, and a mismatched worker who is self-employed is already in U-8 or U-9.

The microdata cannot identify the marginally attached: the extract records why people outside the labor force are not looking, but not whether they could start a job or have looked in the past year. U-7 therefore replaces them with everyone who wants a job, a larger group that contains them, and takes the unemployed and the part time for economic reasons from the official series. Writing u = U/LF and m = MA/LF, U-5 = (u + m)/(1 + m), which gives m = (U-5 − U-3)/(1 − U-5), and with it the part time for economic reasons implied by U-6.

Where Recessions Reach
the Labor Market

The Sahm Rule watches unemployment, so it can see a recession only when the recession reaches the unemployment count. In the United States most do: output falls, firms lay workers off, and the unemployment rate rises within months. In much of Europe and in Japan a downturn is absorbed first in shorter hours, short-time work schemes and workers kept on the payroll, and unemployment rises late, a little, or not at all. This page runs the turning-point rule from Appendix B of the paper on the unemployment rates of 37 peer economies of the United States and scores it against each country’s own recessions, dated from its GDP. The rule works where recessions convert into unemployment, and that depends on how each labor market adjusts more than on how strictly its law protects jobs.

Data and methods

Unemployment. Monthly unemployment rates, seasonally adjusted, as a percent of the labor force: Eurostat’s une_rt_m for the members of the European Union, Norway, Iceland and Switzerland; the OECD’s harmonised unemployment rates for the United States, Canada, Japan, Korea, Australia and Israel; and, for the United Kingdom, whose Eurostat series ends in 2020, the Office for National Statistics series MGSX, for people aged 16 and over. The ONS publishes rolling three-month averages, labelled here by their middle month, so the British indicator is smoother and a little later than the others.

Recessions. For every country but the United States, dated with the Harding and Pagan (2002) version of the Bry–Boschan algorithm on the log of quarterly real GDP: Eurostat’s namq_10_gdp in chain-linked volumes, the OECD’s Quarterly National Accounts outside Europe, and the ONS series ABMI for the United Kingdom. A peak or trough must be the highest or lowest point within two quarters on either side, each phase must last at least two quarters and each full cycle at least five. A recession runs from the first month of the quarter after the peak to the last month of the trough quarter. One check is added to the appendix’s code, from the canonical Bry–Boschan rules: a phase counts as a recession only if GDP is lower at its trough than at its peak.

The rule and the scoring. Appendix B’s configuration: a five-month average, a twelve-month low lagged five months, and a threshold of 0.6 points. Months are scored from January 1995, once the indicator can be computed from data after that date, which puts the first scored month in September 1996 wherever the data go back that far. A recession counts as caught if the alarm sounds between nine months before it starts and nine months after it ends; an alarm episode that touches no such window is a false alarm. Two departures from the appendix’s code: a recession counts only if it reaches the months that can be scored (the appendix also counts recessions that ended before a country’s unemployment data begin, as misses), and a value exactly at the threshold sounds the alarm. Switzerland’s monthly series begins in 2010, Israel’s in 2012 and Iceland’s in 2003, so each is scored on fewer recessions than the rest.

Pass-through and institutions. The Okun coefficient is estimated for each country from 1995 on, by regressing the four-quarter change in its quarterly average unemployment rate on four-quarter growth in real GDP, in log points (Ball, Leigh and Loungani 2017). Employment protection is measured by the OECD’s indicators, version 4, for regular contracts, temporary contracts and collective dismissals, averaged over the years since 1995; the OECD does not cover Bulgaria, Croatia, Cyprus, Malta or Romania. Ireland’s GDP is swollen by the accounts of multinational firms, which weakens its Okun relationship.

Not binary

A recession is not a switch

The NBER’s Business Cycle Dating Committee decides, after the fact, whether the economy as a whole was in a recession and when it began and ended. That is a judgment about the aggregate, and it is binary: a month is either in a recession or it is not.

Workers do not experience the aggregate. A downturn reaches some industries, places and groups of workers months before it reaches others, deepens unevenly and lingers for some long after the official trough. The same indicator, run on different groups, shows that a recession has a composition (who is in it), a distribution (how deep it is for each) and a continuous shape (how much of the labor force is in it at any moment).

What the aggregates said

Never called, but not ruled out

The Dating Committee never declared a recession in this period. But the indicators built to detect recessions in real time were not unanimous. In July 2024 the Sahm Rule reached its 0.5-point threshold. The Michez Rule of Pascal Michaillat and Emmanuel Saez, which requires unemployment to rise and job openings to fall together, entered its recession band in early 2024; in the working-paper version of “Has the Recession Started?”, they put the probability that the US was already in recession at about one in two with August 2024 data, and dated a possible start to March 2024.

Read as a yes-or-no question, the answer was no. Read as a distribution, the more useful question is: for whom was it a downturn, and how much of the labor force was in one?

What this means

Recessions are compositional, distributional and continuous

The vibecession is a useful test case because the aggregate answer and the lived answer diverged. A binary dating rule has to wait until a downturn is broad enough to move the whole economy. A distributional reading can say, month by month, how much of the labor force is in a downturn and who they are, which is closer to what people mean when they say the economy feels like a recession.

None of this says the NBER was wrong. It says that the aggregate is one summary of a distribution, and that indicators built on marginal workers carry information the aggregate averages away.

Forecasting and distribution

More than a forecast

Turning-point rules are usually judged as forecasts: how early do they call a recession, and how often do they raise a false alarm? That question matters, and the indicators on this site are evaluated against it. But the same rules answer a second question that I find more interesting. Applied to the unemployment rates of different groups of workers and different places, they show who is in a recession, when it began for them and how long it lasted.

That is a distributional finding, and distributional findings tend to raise questions of policy. Economics is rightly cautious about prescription, and nothing here is meant as a verdict on how anyone should do their work. What follows are a few general observations about where this kind of analysis might be useful: to the committee that dates the business cycle, to the statistical agencies that measure the labor market, to the researchers who will study recessions in the future, and to policymakers who want support to reach people when they need it.

For the Business Cycle Dating Committee

With gratitude, and a request for a little more detail

The National Bureau of Economic Research’s Business Cycle Dating Committee deserves to be congratulated, both for its work and for the transparency it already provides about the data it examines and the techniques it uses. Its chronology of peaks and troughs, in the tradition of Burns and Mitchell’s Measuring Business Cycles, is the reference point for nearly all research on recessions, including this work. The committee names the monthly indicators it considers, among them real personal income less transfers, nonfarm payroll employment and industrial production; explains each determination when it announces it; and maintains a page of frequently asked questions about its procedure.

That page is also candid that the committee works by judgment. A recession is “a significant decline in economic activity that is spread across the economy and that lasts more than a few months,” and “there is no fixed rule about what measures contribute information to the process or how they are weighted in our decisions.” The peak is “the month in which a variety of economic indicators reach their highest level.” The committee identifies turning points, in other words, without specifying a function that would locate them: how the indicators are combined and weighted, over what window, and against what threshold.

Judgment has served the committee, and the country, well, and there are good reasons not to bind it to a formula. This research may help fill part of the gap from the outside. The indicators at the heart of this work are turning-point functions with every parameter stated, applied in the same way to any group, place or measure. The formulas are written out in full in the methodology, and any reader can change the parameters in the interactive tool.

An astute macroeconomist could also reconstruct a good deal of the committee’s thinking from its members’ own scholarship, which includes careful work on dating turning points with statistical methods, among it Stock and Watson’s (2014) approach to estimating turning points from large data sets. Still, it might be helpful, and a further service to the field, if the committee shared some notes on what its formula generally looks like. The notes need not be exact, and they need not bind its judgment. A general description of the turning-point functions it has in mind, the algorithms or benchmarks it consults, and how much they weigh in its determinations would help researchers interpret its decisions and build on them.

Beyond on and off

A continuous value and a category for each recession

A second suggestion concerns how recessions are recorded. The chronology is binary: a month is either in a recession or it is not. Yet the committee’s own definition is graded. Its procedure notes that “each of the three criteria—depth, diffusion, and duration—needs to be met individually to some degree,” and that “extreme conditions revealed by one criterion may partially offset weaker indications from another.” A binary record keeps the committee’s verdict but sets aside the gradations behind it.

One way to keep them would be to record each recession, or each month of one, with a continuous value rather than a 0 or a 1: an index of intensity running from zero to one that summarizes the depth, diffusion and duration the committee already weighs. Values near zero would mark contractions at the edge of the definition, and values near one the most severe. How much of the labor force is in a downturn in a given month, the reading this site applies to the years since 2022 in its comments on the vibecession, is one natural candidate, since a share lies between zero and one by construction.

Alongside that value, each recession could be assigned a category describing its origin, drawn from a small, documented set: financial, monetary, energy and other supply shocks, public health, and so on. Together the two would convey much more than a date. A recession recorded in the financial category with an intensity of 0.7 would describe a severe downturn originating in the financial sector; one recorded as a supply shock with an intensity of 0.3, a milder contraction following a disruption to supply. Each piece of information bears on how an episode should be studied and on which responses are likely to help.

Committee members have already shown how such gradations can be measured. Christina and David Romer’s (2017) narrative series on financial distress in 24 OECD countries grades distress every half-year on a scale from 0 to 15, in categories that run from a credit disruption to an extreme crisis, rather than recording crises as simply present or absent. We will likely never be able to measure every component of every past recession. But American statistical practice is closely watched and widely emulated around the world, and beginning this work, perhaps even back-dating continuous values and categories for earlier recessions, would be a lasting gift to future researchers, here and abroad.

For the Bureau of Labor Statistics

Measuring the margins of the labor market

Much of this work is possible only because of the Bureau of Labor Statistics. The Current Population Survey, which it sponsors with the Census Bureau, yields unemployment rates every month for a wide range of groups, and the Bureau publishes six alternative measures of labor underutilization, U-1 through U-6, that this site studies in its pages on underemployment. The microdata behind these statistics are public. That openness is a public good, and it deserves more recognition than it gets.

Three further steps might be especially valuable. The first would build on those alternative measures toward a more expansive understanding of slack. The broadest of them, U-6, adds to the unemployed the people marginally attached to the labor force, who want and are available for a job and have looked for one in the past year, and those working part time for economic reasons. It still leaves out many people with unused capacity to work: people who want a job but have not looked for one in the past year, the self-employed at the margins of independent and gig work, workers whose jobs do not use their skills or education, and adults in their prime working years who have left the labor force, some of whom would return if suitable work were available.

Economists have proposed ways to capture more of this, such as the nonemployment index of Hornstein, Kudlyak and Lange (2014), which counts everyone without a job and accounts for differences in their attachment to the labor force. A seventh, more comprehensive measure of slack, published alongside the existing six, would give a fuller account of how much of the country’s capacity to work goes unused, and of who bears that loss, in recessions and between them.

The second would reach further into the margins of the labor market: for instance, statistics on unemployed people whose previous work was informal, paid in cash or “under the table.” Because such workers often sit at the edge of the labor market, their employment might be more cyclical than that of workers in formal jobs, and their marginality might show signs of weakness sooner. Measuring them, even occasionally and in a supplement to the survey, could give an earlier and fuller picture of how a downturn begins.

The third would be to publish unemployment rates by family income as a standard data series. The survey already asks households about their family income, and this work uses those answers to estimate unemployment by income decile each month. The results are among its clearest: recessions begin sooner and last longer for workers in the poorest families. Regularly published rates by family income would let researchers and policymakers follow this dimension of inequality as routinely as they now follow unemployment by age, sex, race and education.

For policymakers

From a stimulus trigger to targeted support

Claudia Sahm designed her rule as a policy instrument. In Recession Ready, published in 2019 by the Hamilton Project and the Washington Center for Equitable Growth, she proposed it as a trigger for automatic stimulus payments to households, so that support would arrive automatically and early in a downturn. A clear, timely signal tied to a response is the idea this work tries to extend.

Policymakers who want to soften recessions face questions that a single national date cannot answer. Some are spatial: recessions reach some counties months before others, and local labor markets follow patterns of their own. Some concern inequality within the country, where the timing of recession differs sharply by income, education and race. Some concern particular sectors and the consequences a downturn brings to them. The same turning-point logic, applied to granular data, can speak to each: telling a state or a county when its own labor market has turned, showing which groups a downturn has reached first, or marking when the workers of a particular sector need support.

Used this way, the turning point that Sahm proposed as a national trigger can inform a wider range of multivariate, socioeconomic and spatial problems, from narrowing educational disparities to shoring up hard-hit sectors. The hope is a modest one: that better-timed and better-targeted information leads, over time, to better support for workers when employment falters, from unemployment insurance to the programs that help them back into work.

How the field has evolved

A rule for stimulus became a research program

2019
The Sahm Rule

Claudia Sahm, then an economist at the Federal Reserve Board, proposed the rule in “Direct Stimulus Payments to Individuals,” a chapter of Recession Ready, published by the Hamilton Project and the Washington Center for Equitable Growth. It was designed as a trigger for automatic stimulus payments: a recession signal when the three-month average unemployment rate rises half a percentage point above its low over the previous twelve months. The Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis now publishes it in real time on FRED, and Sahm writes about the economy at Stay-At-Home Macro.

2024
The Michez Rule

Pascal Michaillat and Emmanuel Saez added the other side of the labor market. Their rule, in “Has the Recession Started?”, takes the smaller of two indicators: the Sahm-style rise in unemployment and the fall in job openings from their recent peak. A recession is signaled when that minimum enters a band from 0.30 to 0.80 percentage points: at the bottom of the band a recession has probably begun, and at the top it is near certain. Requiring both signals at once guards against a rise in unemployment that comes from more people entering the labor force while employers are still hiring. The paper was published in the Oxford Bulletin of Economics and Statistics in 2025.

2025
Validation and classifiers

Michaillat has since built a broader framework for testing these indicators. “Early and Accurate Recession Detection Using Classifiers on the Anticipation-Precision Frontier” searches across many rules built from unemployment and vacancy data, keeps those that detect recessions earliest without false alarms, and turns them into a recession probability. Related work with Saez on the full-employment rate of unemployment, u* = √uv, grounds the same labor-market data in theory.

Now
Underemployment and disaggregation

The Modified Sahm Rule treats every convention of the Sahm Rule as a parameter and applies it to any labor-market series. Run on U-6, it tends to move before the headline rate, consistent with hours being cut before workers are laid off. Run on groups of workers, it shows that recessions arrive at different times for different people.

The paper

Works Like a Sahm: Recession Indicators and the Sahm Rule

Thomas Ash and Jerry Nickelsburg ask whether the Sahm Rule works as a recession predictor. They take two approaches: a look back at historical recessions, and stochastic simulations from a small vector autoregression (VAR) in real GDP, prices and unemployment, in which each simulated path is checked against the rule. They conclude that the rule is a poor predictor of future recessions because it focuses on unemployment and so misses shocks that start outside the labor market, and that it should be kept to the purpose its creator intended. The paper is a useful contribution: the VAR framework is a clean way to ask how a labor-market indicator responds to different kinds of shocks.

Re-running that framework, though, the headline result turns out to rest on how the rule was coded rather than on the rule itself. Holding the authors’ own model and data fixed and implementing the rule as Sahm defines it, the rule picks up roughly three times as many of the simulated recessions, including those that begin outside the labor market.

The specification

Three differences from the rule as defined

Four months, not three

The average is taken over observations k = i − 3 through i, which is four months. The Sahm Rule uses a three-month average.

Zero, not the year’s low

The rule is applied to the impulse response itself, the deviation of unemployment from its no-shock path, and compared with zero rather than with its lowest three-month average over the previous twelve months. The unemployment level the script builds for this purpose is never used.

Shocks scaled twice

Each shock is drawn from the VAR’s covariance matrix and then multiplied again by that matrix’s Cholesky factor, which changes the size and mix of the simulated shocks. Orthogonal draws, scaled once, give shocks with the covariance the model estimated.

The first two make the test stricter than the Sahm Rule: a four-month average that has to clear half a point on its own, with no credit for falling from a recent low. The third changes which shocks the simulation produces. None of them is unusual in a working script, and catching this kind of detail is what replication is for.

Simulating…

What holds up

An indicator, not a forecast

The paper’s broader point stands, and it is consistent with Sahm’s own description of the rule: a turning-point indicator keyed to realized labor-market slack is not a one-month-ahead forecaster, and it tends to become informative a few months into a downturn, later when the shock starts outside the labor market. That is a statement about propagation. Shocks show up first where they start and later in hours, hiring and unemployment. It applies to the Modified Sahm Rule as well, which is why the series on this site uses it to date and describe downturns rather than to predict them.

The frontier

Early or precise: every perfect classifier, one dot each

Each dot is a rule built from one labor-market series with its own smoothing window, comparison window and threshold, that flags exactly the recessions in the training period and nothing else. Building classifiers…

What the search is for

Labor theory, not only statistical fit, should pick the indicator

In The Inequality of Recession, several machine-learning techniques described in the paper narrow this space of rules to a smaller subset of classifiers. The search is useful, but its main lesson is not a single winning parameter set. Across every paper in the series, the same pattern recurs: workers who are more marginal in the labor market are less firmly attached to it, so their unemployment and underemployment move first when demand weakens.

Earlier

Any measure of labor-market vulnerability (underemployment, Black unemployment, the lowest income deciles, workers without a degree) tends to turn before the aggregate rate.

Noisier

The same weak attachment makes these series more volatile, so they can also raise more false alarms or need more smoothing.

A theoretical choice

How much noise to accept for an earlier signal is a question about how recessions spread through the labor market, not only an optimization over statistical error.

That is why the frontier above highlights the Black Sahm Rule and the U-6 Modified Sahm Rule beside the original: they are not arbitrary points in a grid, but indicators chosen because theory says where a downturn should show up first. The “Works Like a Sahm” specification is shown for comparison, translated to historical data (see Replications).

How it works

From a single rule to a search over rules

Michaillat (2025) constructs 95,832 indicators from unemployment and vacancy data, varying smoothing, curvature, turning-point windows and how the two series are mixed, then keeps only “perfect classifiers”: indicator-threshold pairs that detect exactly the right number of recessions in a training period. Among those, the anticipation-precision frontier collects the rules that cannot be made earlier without being made less precise. This page applies the same logic to the Modified Sahm Rule family: for each series, smoothing window k, comparison window m and threshold α, it records when the rule first crosses its threshold in each episode and compares those dates with NBER recession starts.

Method notes

Indicator: the k-month average of the series minus its minimum over the comparison window (the current month and the previous m months, or the previous m months only). A recession signal is an upward crossing of α while no signal is active; a signal ends when the indicator falls back below α. (Michaillat ends a signal when his indicators return to zero; for Sahm-type indicators, which rarely reach zero between closely spaced recessions such as 1980 and 1981–82, falling below the threshold is the standard reading.) A series is included when it starts at least three years before the first recession in the training period. A classifier is perfect when the number of signals in the training period equals the number of NBER recessions and each signal falls within 24 months of the recession it is paired with, in order. Recession start is the month after the NBER peak, as in Michaillat. Error = signal month minus recession start; the horizontal axis is its standard deviation across recessions and the vertical axis its mean. Thresholds are searched on the grid shown; neighboring thresholds that give identical dates are shown once.

Highlighted rules: the Sahm Rule (U-3, k = 3, previous 12 months, α = 0.5); the U-6 Modified Sahm Rule (same, on U-6); the Sahm Rule on Black unemployment; and the check implemented in “Works Like a Sahm.” In their simulations that check is the four-month average of the unemployment response compared with zero, the no-shock path; on historical data it is translated here as the four-month average of the unemployment rate minus its level in the month just before that window, with α = 0.5. Every highlighted rule is plotted and labeled; one that misses a recession or raises a false alarm is drawn hollow, and its position uses only the recessions it detects. Over 1994–2021 the Sahm Rule’s one false alarm is July 2003, during the jobless recovery; over the longer periods it also signals in late 1959.

The indicator: Modified Sahm Rule formulas

The Modified Sahm Rule consists of two components: the minuend, \( \lambda_{i,t} \), and the subtrahend, \( \gamma_{j,t} \). The Modified Sahm Rule is defined as:

\[ \text{Sahm}(\lambda_{i,t}, \gamma_{j,t}) = \lambda_{i,t} - \gamma_{j,t}, \]

where:

\(\lambda_{i,t} = \overline{y}^{k\text{-month}}_{i,t} = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{j=0}^{k} y_{i,t-j}\)\(\gamma_{j,t} = \min_{t-11 \leq k \leq t} \overline{y}^{k\text{-month}}_{j,k}\)

The recession indicator \( R_t \) is triggered when the Sahm Rule exceeds a threshold \( \alpha \):

\[ R_t = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \text{Sahm}(\cdot) > \alpha, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \]

Note the following:

  • \( k \) is typically set to 3 months, but the function can be smoothed over any \( k \)-month average. Generally, subcategories of unemployment with increased volatility require more smoothing.
  • \( \alpha \) is generally set to 0.5, but this parameter can be adjusted to establish upper and lower bounds, such as 0.3 and 0.8, as discussed in Michaillat and Saez (2024).
  • Indices \( i \) and \( j \) allow for comparing different unemployment measures (e.g., \( i \neq j \), such as comparing Hispanic women unemployment or the unemployment rate in Cocke County, Tennessee against the baseline \( U_3 \) as the subtrahend, creating a Relative Sahm Rule). This formulation can also be extended to include job vacancy or any labor utilization measure.
